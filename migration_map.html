The seven lines

Each family below is framed on its own movements rather than on a shared map, so the shape of the frame is part of what it tells you: the Burrells reach Indiana and Kansas, the Dillards barely leave two counties, and the Connors have no map at all.

Black

40 people in the register, born 1698–1898 · 11 recorded movements, drawn as 8 arcs · crossed Co. Londonderry to Dauphin Co, PA by 1763 · crossed Co. Londonderry to Mecklenburg Co, NC by 1818

Scots-Irish Presbyterians out of County Londonderry. Robert Black crossed from Belfast to Lancaster County, Pennsylvania; his son James, the immigrant, was born in Ulster about 1720 and died at ninety-eight in Mecklenburg County, North Carolina in 1818. Joseph Augustus Black — the family's proven fifth-great-grandfather — was born in Dauphin County, Pennsylvania in 1763 and buried eighty years later in a Presbyterian churchyard in Columbia, South Carolina. The line's arcs here are the Wagon Road in miniature: Ulster to Dauphin County, then Dauphin County south into Abbeville and the South Carolina upcountry.

The Ulster origin was very nearly lost. A pedigree that appeared to prove it turned out on inspection to belong to a neighbouring Black family of Antreville — but the two families share a root, and the road home ran through a different son. Both James and Joseph Augustus were remembered as Revolutionary veterans.

Norton

26 people in the register, born 1687–1928 · 16 recorded movements, drawn as 13 arcs · crossed Armagh to Fairfax Co, VA by 1740 · crossed Armagh to Randolph Co, NC by 1778

Quakers from Armagh — the Society of Friends, who would not swear oaths, would not doff their hats to gentlemen and would not fight, and who were fined and jailed on both sides of the Atlantic for it. Edward Norton, born in Armagh in 1687, crossed with the Friends into Pennsylvania and then to the Quaker settlement at Providence Meeting in Guilford County, North Carolina, where he died in 1778. Only later, pushing on into the southern mountains, did the Nortons drift toward the Methodist and Baptist churches of the frontier.

The heaviest single Norton arc on this map is the one that named a place. In the early 1820s Barak Pickeral Norton's family crossed the Blue Ridge out of South Carolina's Pickens District into Whiteside Cove and Cashiers Valley — children born in Pickens through 1821, in the Carolina mountains by 1828 — and the Norton community of Jackson County is on the map because of it. Barak lived to 92, his wife Mary Nicholson to 95.

Who moved: William Norton (b.1740) (1740–1821), Barak Pickeral Norton (1777–1869), Edward Norton (immigrant, 1718) (1716–1778), Rodric Norton (1808–1866), Sarah Whiteside Norton (1828–1902), David Norton (1835–1912), John Burruss Norton (1856–1937), Octavia Norton (1851–1932), Marcella Norton (1853–1931), Griffin Norton (1868–1938), Audrey Juanita Norton (1926–1993)

Dillard

16 people in the register, born 1680–1906 · 16 recorded movements, drawn as 7 arcs

The Dillards come down the Virginia side: King & Queen County to Pittsylvania in the 1760s, then on into North Carolina. Their heaviest arc is late and short — nine people out of Pickens District into North Carolina around 1888.

D.L. Dillard farmed in Webster township with at least eight children, recorded in the 1870 census only by their initials — among them 'J.R.L.', age 5, who is James, Bertha's father. After D.L. died his sons spread along the Tuckasegee into Sylva township, where by 1910 'Dillard' had become a common local given name. James took his branch to Macon County by 1910, and there his daughter Bertha met Leonard Norton.

Burrell

19 people in the register, born 1715–1824 · 24 recorded movements, drawn as 17 arcs · crossed Fife to South Carolina by 1715 · crossed Fife to Anson Co, NC by 1775

Lowland Scots from Fife, and the widest-travelling line on the map. They reach the Carolinas in the eighteenth century, gather in Greenville District, and then scatter further than anyone else in the family: five people out of Greenville into North Carolina about 1795, three on to Harrison County, Indiana — where Butler Burrell, born about 1795, had taken his family by 1820 and where he was reported drowned before the spring of 1855 — later branches into Rabun County, Georgia, and descendants as far west as Kansas.

The Burrells are also the family's Gettysburg story, and it belongs to a different Butler: the elder Butler's nephew, born 1826, a son of Mark Burrell, and a collateral rather than a direct ancestor. Two Butlers and three Brights run through these generations, which is how readily this family confuses its own record.

That younger Butler was killed on 3 July 1863 in the low ground between Devil's Den and Little Round Top that the soldiers called the Valley of Death. His brother Bright, riding cavalry, found him mortally wounded, dragged him behind a boulder to shield them both from the fire, wrapped the body in a saddle blanket, and had to ride on to catch his unit. Butler lies in an unmarked grave on the field; a marble monument stands for him in the Burrell Cemetery in Rabun County. The family long told that his wife Malinda, sitting on her porch that day, saw a whirlwind come up the road and cried out that something had happened to Butler — at, they reckoned afterwards, the very hour he died.

Connor

13 people in the register, born 1754–1925 · 0 recorded movements

The Connors draw no line on this map, and that is the finding rather than a gap. Every movement recorded for them runs between two places inside a single county. They are the family that stayed.

Where they stayed was the Tuckasegee country. In 1910 Frank Connor, a widowed farmer, was raising six children in Mountain township, Jackson County; two dwellings down, the widower Joshua Adams was raising four alone. Alec Connor and Emma Adams — the boy at No. 42 and the girl at No. 44 — married around 1918. By April 1950 Alec was driving a truck for a grocery on Highway 107 south of Sylva, a few doors from Leonard Norton and a quarter-mile from Will Dillard, who trucked wood for the paper mill.

No movement to draw.

Hancock

8 people in the register, born 1801–1930 · 6 recorded movements, drawn as 4 arcs

A North Carolina Piedmont family, and the one coloured line here that never touches the Wagon Road or the mountains. Reuben Hancock was in Montgomery County when he married the young widow Elizabeth Thomas on 21 December 1865; their son Burrell Franklin Hancock, born 1867, is the direct ancestor. The movement drawn here is short and late — Montgomery County into Richmond County by the 1920s, and on to Randolph and Moore.

Elizabeth was carried in the family record for years as 'Elizabeth Freeman'. She was born Elizabeth Thomas, daughter of a widowed Elizabeth Thomas of Montgomery County; Freeman was the name of her first husband, Noah, who died during the Civil War years. She brought his two small daughters into the Hancock marriage, and in the next census they appear under their stepfather's name — which is how a surname that was never hers came to stand as her own for a century.

Who moved: Lucretia Elon Hancock (1928–2019), John David Hancock (1899–1994), Vella Mae Hancock (1930–2010), Burrell Franklin Hancock (1867–1953)

Adams

9 people in the register, born 1827–1899 · 3 recorded movements, drawn as 3 arcs

The Adamses enter from Georgia, and enter twice. Emly Adams and her daughter Martha came north; Martha's daughter Meeta was born in Georgia in 1864, deep in the Civil War, before the family appears in Hamburg township, North Carolina, where Martha is wife to David Norton by 1880 with her mother in the household.

Separately, Banister Adams was farming in North Carolina by about 1859, and by 1880 lived two doors from David Norton. One Georgia clan feeding both sides of the tree: Martha into the Nortons and — probably — Banister's son Joshua fathering Emma, who married Alec Connor.

Who moved: Joshua Adams (1862–1936), Banister Adams (1832–1910)

Other families

192 people in the register, born 1019–1907 · 38 recorded movements, drawn as 33 arcs · crossed Wiltshire to Charles City, VA by 1616 · crossed Shropshire to Calvert Co, MD by 1658 · crossed England to Lancaster Co, VA by 1672 · and 3 more crossings

Every surname that is not one of the six, drawn in the neutral ink. It is the largest group on the map and the oldest: the English arrivals into the Chesapeake a century before the Scots-Irish tide — Thomas Pruitt out of Wiltshire to Virginia in the 1610s, Francis Billingsley out of Shropshire to Maryland by the 1650s — along with the Brownlees of Armagh, the Hancocks, the Nicholsons and the rest of the families these six married into.

A surname sits here because the map has no thread to follow, not because the family matters less. Several of them move more often than the coloured lines do.

The Wagon Road is the historical route from Philadelphia through the Shenandoah Valley to the Carolina backcountry — the road the Blacks, Nortons and Brownlees came south on — and not any single journey. Living family members are not plotted, and places are approximate county or town centres, so a line shows that a family moved rather than the path they walked. 11 of the arcs are drawn dashed: every person who made those moves is graded speculative in the register, so the descent is recorded but not proven. That includes the deep English line back through the Mowbrays, which should not be read as established. The Connor line draws nothing here, which is itself the finding: every move recorded for them is between two places inside one county, so there is no distance to draw. 1 movement is not dated in the register and cannot be placed in the sequence, so it is left out of this panel rather than parked at an invented year.
